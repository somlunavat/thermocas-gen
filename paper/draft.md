## Title

**No denominator: putting a scale on growth-temperature conditioning in
protein language models**

Alternates:

- *How much is a growth-temperature label worth? A corpus-only scale, and what a threshold throws away*
- *The label is a free parameter: choosing growth-temperature bins before training*

Note on the 2026-11-02 retitle. The previous title asked "how much can a
growth-temperature label help?" and promised "a corpus-only bound on
conditioning gain". The paper no longer delivers a bound anyone should use to
*choose* a label -- section 5.2d refutes that against a trivial baseline -- and
a title promising one would not survive review. What it does deliver is a
scale, and the argument that the field needs one.

## Abstract

Six recent systems condition protein language models on growth temperature,
across Science Advances, NeurIPS, ICML, Scientific Reports and Nature. None
reports how much its label could have been worth. The best-resourced of them,
trained on 96M sequence-temperature pairs, ablates its temperature objective
and reports the difference as +0.106 Spearman on thermostability assays and
-0.004 on activity assays -- numbers that cannot be compared across papers or
against any property of the corpus they were obtained on. We supply the
missing scale and measure what it is worth. Mutual information between residue
identity and label, computed from residue counts in CPU-minutes, puts the value
of a label in perplexity units on the corpus the model trains on; at a
database's native temperature resolution it bounds every coarser binning of
that database's labels, because mutual information cannot fall when a partition
is refined. Across three prokaryotic corpora that envelope is +0.49% to +0.97%
in perplexity, and the two-class threshold every surveyed system uses reaches
only 23% to 30% of it. The estimate is unigram, so it is a lower bound on what
a contextual model extracts and not a ceiling on it: ten of our sixteen runs
exceed their own, by up to 1.56x.
We then measure what a model actually extracts, in seventeen GPU runs that
differ only in how the label is defined -- byte-identical sequences, clusters
and splits, so the comparison isolates label definition from corpus
composition, which nothing in the related work does. How the label is binned
is worth a factor of 3.9 to 4.2 in measured conditioning gain, on three
corpora and two backbones, with margins an order of magnitude above the
shuffle noise floor; thresholds below 35 degrees C give a small fraction of
what is available above 40. The statistic is a usable scale and a poor
selector, and we establish the second half rather than hiding it: on a corpus
chosen in advance so that the predictors disagree, with the outcome
pre-registered, a baseline that reads nothing from any corpus ranks four label
definitions perfectly while the statistic ranks them no better than chance
(+1.000 against +0.000; the pre-registered comparison is negative at more than
six standard deviations on both splits). The failure is predictable from label
entropy, which is why we recommend reporting it: mutual information is
maximised at an even marginal, so the bound inflates wherever a threshold
splits a corpus evenly, which on a reviewed-protein corpus means wherever it
separates organisms annotated at 37 degrees C from everything else.

Word count target for submission: ~200, and the draft above is ~300. What
compresses: the pre-registration detail and the backbone result. What does
not: the +0.106, the envelope range, the 23-30%, and the 3.9-4.2x.

## 1. Introduction

Protein language models are now routinely asked to generate sequences with a
target property, and thermostability is among the most sought after: a
thermostable enzyme is cheaper to ship, store and run. The mechanism of control
is almost always the same. The organism a sequence came from has a documented
growth temperature, that temperature is reduced to a label, and the label is
supplied to the model as a token, an embedding, an auxiliary regression target
or a steering vector. At least five recent systems do some version of this,
across *Science Advances*, *NeurIPS*, *ICML*, *Scientific Reports* and
*Nature*.

The effects they report are small, and they are reported without a scale. No
paper in this literature states how much its label *could* have been worth on
the corpus it used, so a reader cannot tell whether a method extracted most of
the available signal or a tenth of it, nor whether a competing number obtained
on a different corpus reflects better work or an easier corpus.

The clearest illustration is the best-resourced system in the area. PRIME
trains on 96M sequence-temperature pairs and is, of the six systems we
surveyed, the only one that ablates its temperature objective at all. Removing
it costs 0.106 Spearman on twenty thermostability assay datasets (0.439 to
0.333) and nothing on eleven activity datasets (0.414 to 0.418). That is a
careful ablation and it is still unreadable as evidence about labels: 0.106
Spearman over a particular assay set has no upper bound, depends on the
mutation distribution and the scoring scheme, and cannot be set against
anything computable from the 96M pairs themselves. The asymmetry between the
two assay families is suggestive -- a growth-temperature label ought to help
with thermostability and not with activity -- and nobody can currently check
it against a scale. The quantities this field compares have no denominator.

This paper supplies that denominator, measures what a model actually extracts
against it, and establishes precisely how far it can be trusted. The quantity
is cheap: the mutual information between residue identity and label, computed
from residue counts over the same corpus the model trains on, in CPU-minutes.
Because mutual information cannot fall when a label partition is refined, the
same quantity evaluated at a temperature database's native resolution
upper-bounds *every* binning of that database's labels -- an envelope against
which any particular choice can be read. On three prokaryotic corpora that
envelope is +0.49% to +0.97% in perplexity, and the two-class threshold used
by every system in section 2 reaches 23% to 30% of it.

To find out, we hold everything except the label fixed. Our sweep variants are
*relabelings*: `temp_bin` is recomputed from the recorded growth temperature in
memory at startup, so every variant trains on byte-identical sequences,
clusters and splits, and only the meaning of the control token changes. Across
seventeen runs on three corpora and two backbones we find that **how the label
is defined is worth a factor of 3.9 to 4.2 in measured conditioning gain**,
from +0.070% at cold thresholds to +0.273% on a plateau between 45 and 65
degrees C on the primary corpus, and by comparable factors on the other two,
with per-run margins an order of magnitude above the shuffle noise floor. Label
definition is a free parameter that nobody tunes and it is worth more than most
of the modelling choices made around it.

The denominator does not work well enough to recommend, and establishing that
carefully is the rest of the paper. On our two species-balanced corpora it
predicts the ordering of label definitions when the mutual information is
conditioned on sequence family, and not otherwise. On a third corpus, built
from the same database without species selection, it predicts nothing: the
bound and the measured gain are anti-correlated, because mutual information
rises with class balance and on that corpus the balanced thresholds are the
semantically empty ones. And a baseline that reads nothing from any corpus --
distance from the middle of the temperature range -- beats it everywhere we
looked, including on the corpus we chose in advance to give the statistic its
best chance. We report these as results rather than limitations, because what
the field needs is a denominator that works, and the most useful thing we can
say about this candidate is precisely where and why it fails.

We make no claim that the measure estimates achievable gain, and none that it
ranks label definitions robustly. It is a lower bound computed from unigram
statistics; ten of our sixteen runs exceed their own bound, by up to 1.56x,
as a model with context should. What we claim is that the field is missing a denominator, that this is a
cheap and testable candidate for one, and that this is precisely how far it
goes.

**Contributions.**

1. A relabeling-only experimental design that isolates label definition from
   every other corpus property. Nothing in the related work does this; its
   comparisons confound label definition with corpus composition.
2. The measurement that label definition is worth 3.9x to 4.2x in conditioning
   gain, over seventeen runs on three corpora and two backbones, each verified
   against a shuffled-token control and a cluster-disjoint test split.
3. An envelope over all binnings of a temperature database's labels, with the
   finding that the conventional two-class threshold reaches 23-30% of it, and
   a Miller-Madow bias correction reported beside every figure because the
   bias grows with exactly the refinement that raises the estimate.
4. A characterisation of when a corpus-only statistic predicts which
   definition is better and when it does not, with the failure traced to class
   balance -- a mechanism predicted in advance from the fact that mutual
   information is maximised at an even marginal, and strong enough on an
   unselected corpus to anti-correlate the bound with measured gain at
   Pearson -0.93.
5. A pre-registered negative result: on a corpus chosen in advance so that the
   two disagree, a baseline that reads nothing from any corpus ranks label
   definitions perfectly and the corpus-wide statistic ranks them no better
   than chance. With two further documented limits -- no ranking across
   granularities, and a measured optimum that is a plateau wide enough that
   locating it precisely has little value.
6. A worked negative case in which two corpus gates rule out a Cas9
   conditioning experiment for the price of a CPU afternoon.

*Writing note, and the central honesty requirement of the paper:* the
introduction must not promise that the statistic estimates achievable gain or
that it ranks robustly. A reviewer who reaches section 6 and finds it
contradicting the framing above would be right to say so, so paragraphs five
and six above are load-bearing and must not be trimmed for length.

## 2. Related work

Temperature conditioning of protein generators is crowded, and that crowding is
this paper's argument rather than its competition. Six representative systems:

| Work | Venue | Approach | Training scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| PRIME | Sci. Adv. 2024 | MLM + sequence-level OGT regression, coupled by a correlation loss | ~96M sequence-OGT pairs |
| ThermoFormer | NeurIPS 2024 workshop | Temperature-aware representation learning | ~96M OGT-annotated sequences |
| Steering PLMs | ICML 2025 | Activation-addition steering, >70 vs <50 degrees C | ESM2-650M, ESM3-open, ProLLaMA-7B |
| NOMELT | Sci. Rep. 2025 | Meso-to-thermo seq2seq translation | 4M pairs, 2.7k Pfam families |
| OpenCRISPR-1 | Nature 2025 | ProGen2-base fine-tuned on the CRISPR-Cas Atlas | 4M generated sequences |
| TemStaPro | Bioinformatics 2024 | ProtT5 embeddings to a thermostability classifier | predictor, not generator |

These systems differ in architecture, in supervision and in how the label
enters the model, and each reports a gain over its own baseline. What none of
them reports is how much their label could have been worth on their corpus, and
none varies the label definition while holding the corpus fixed.

One of the six ablates the temperature objective itself, and its numbers are
the clearest statement of the problem this paper addresses:

| PRIME, zero-shot mutant effect (Spearman) | thermostability, 20 datasets | activity, 11 datasets |
|---|---|---|
| without the OGT task | 0.333 | 0.418 |
| with the OGT task | **0.439** | 0.414 |

The label is worth +0.106 on one assay family and -0.004 on the other. We cite
this as the field's best available evidence about what a growth-temperature
label contributes, and as the reason that evidence is not enough: the units
are downstream, unbounded above, and specific to an assay set, so the figure
cannot be compared with any other paper's or with anything computable from the
96M pairs PRIME trained on. Where a
threshold is stated it is 45 degrees C, or a hot/cold split at 70 and 50, taken
as given rather than chosen. The design in section 4 exists because these
comparisons confound the label with the corpus.

Two debts should be stated plainly rather than hedged. **The auxiliary growth-
temperature regression head in our configuration is PRIME's idea, not ours.**
And **PRIME and ThermoFormer train on roughly 340x more labelled sequence than
we do**: ~96M annotated pairs against our 311,987 sequences. No framing in
which corpus scale is our contribution survives that comparison and we do not
attempt one. Our contribution is a measurement over a design they do not run,
not a larger model.

Searching outward from this literature -- conditional protein generation,
controllable text generation, information-theoretic limits on conditioning, and
label informativeness -- we found nothing that measures the achievable gain from
a conditioning label. The nearest neighbours address adjacent problems.
Variational bounds on mutual information (Poole et al., ICML 2019) are concerned
with estimating MI in high dimensions, not with budgeting a label; our estimate
is a direct count over a discrete alphabet and needs none of that machinery.
Mutual information has been used as a *training mechanism*, capped or maximised
in the loss (arXiv 2203.02055), which is the opposite direction of use. Entropy
reduction as a measure of annotation informativeness appears in vision (arXiv
1812.10358), the closest thing to our framing that we found, but on label sets
rather than on a generative model's conditioning budget. And label-aware
generation in homologous families (arXiv 2507.15651) reports gains plateauing
past roughly 10^3 labels, evaluated by discriminator AUC rather than by an
information quantity. We claim novelty for the question, not for the estimator.

Finally, a caution borrowed from *Off-Manifold Collapse in Guided Protein
Language Models* (arXiv 2608.18597): they report that harder steering lowers
ESMFold pLDDT under both solubility and thermostability steering. We cite this
where we state that structure prediction is a **validity filter** in this work
and not a thermostability assay, which is the one place a reader might
otherwise over-read our use of it.

## 3. The measure

For residues $r$ drawn from the twenty-letter alphabet and a label $b$ assigned
to each sequence from its organism's growth temperature, the quantity is the
mutual information between the two, in nats, estimated by counting:

$$ I(r; b) = H(r) - \sum_b P(b)\, H(r \mid b) $$

A model that knew nothing about a sequence except its residue marginal, and was
then told $b$, would see its per-residue cross-entropy fall by $I(r;b)$, so we
report throughout the relative perplexity gain this corresponds to,
$1 - e^{-I}$, in percent. Every number in this paper called a *bound* or a
*ceiling* is that figure.

We report two aggregations. The **corpus-wide** form counts residues over the
whole corpus. The **within-cluster** form computes $I(r; b \mid \text{family})$,
averaging over MMseqs2 clusters and so asking what the label adds *given the
homologs* -- which is the question the model actually faces, since it sees a
sequence's relatives in training.

Six things have to be said about this quantity, in roughly the order a reviewer
will punish their absence.

**The two aggregations are not interchangeable, and this paper's result turns on
which is used.** The within-cluster form predicts measured gain; the
corpus-wide form does not (section 5.1c). We state this here, where both are
defined, rather than letting a reader reach section 5 believing the choice was
cosmetic. There is also an a priori reason to expect it: mutual information is
maximised when the marginal over $b$ is even, so any threshold that happens to
split the corpus evenly is flattered by the corpus-wide form whether or not the
two classes differ in composition. Conditioning on family does not remove that
effect but it substantially reduces it, because within a family the class
masses are whatever the family's organisms happen to be. This prediction was
made before the eight-threshold runs and section 5.1c confirms it.

**This is a lower bound on what a model can extract, not an upper bound.** The
estimate is unigram: it sees residue composition and nothing else. A language
model has context and can exploit substitutions, motif placement and length
that no residue histogram can represent. Ten of our sixteen runs exceed their
own bound -- ratios across the sixteen span 0.31x to 1.56x -- which is the
expected behaviour and not an error. The measure therefore
cannot support any claim that conditioning has saturated, and we make none.

**It is nevertheless a scale**, and that is the use we put it to. It is computed
from the same corpus the model trains on, so it reports how strongly the label
co-varies with composition *there*, which is exactly the thing missing when two
papers compare conditioning gains obtained on different corpora.

**Monotonicity under refinement is both a limit and a use.** Mutual
information cannot fall when the label partition is refined, so splitting two
classes into four cannot lower it. This is a fact about the quantity rather
than an implementation defect, and it is why the measure cannot compare label
definitions that differ in granularity (section 6.2).

The same fact gives the quantity its most useful form. Evaluated with one
class per distinct temperature the database records, it upper-bounds every
binning of those labels: no threshold, quantile scheme or multi-class scheme
can exceed it. We call that the corpus's **envelope** and report it in section
5.1b. It is what a group with a corpus and no GPU budget can compute to find
out what their label could be worth at best.

Monotonicity is also why the envelope needs a bias correction before it is
quoted. A plug-in estimate of mutual information from counts is biased upward
by about (|R| - 1)(|B| - 1) / 2N nats, and |B| at native resolution is 47 to
77 rather than two, so the bias grows with exactly the refinement that raises
the estimate. We report the Miller-Madow correction beside every figure. At
these corpus sizes it is 0.000 to 0.005 percentage points against envelopes of
0.5 to 1.0, which is worth establishing rather than assuming.

**Binning convention: upper-exclusive**, so $\text{ogt} < \text{edge}$ is the
cold class. 45 degrees C is a common exact value in growth-temperature
databases -- it is a catalogue figure, not a measurement -- so the convention
moves thousands of sequences between classes and must be stated.

**Growth temperature** is the label; **sampling temperature** is a decoding
setting. The second appears nowhere in this paper. We state this once, here,
because the collision of terms is unavoidable in this subfield and silently
confusing.

Cost: the full eleven-threshold sweep over 113.8M residues is CPU-minutes, on
one core. This matters to the argument -- a denominator that cost a GPU run
would not be worth computing.

## 4. Experimental design

**A sweep variant is a relabeling, not a second corpus.** `temp_bin` is
recomputed from the recorded growth temperature `ogt_c` in memory at startup,
so every variant in a sweep trains on byte-identical sequences, clusters and
splits; only the meaning of the control token changes. This is a small methods
contribution and we state it as one. It is also the reason our comparisons are
interpretable where the related work's are not: there, changing the label means
rebuilding the corpus, and the resulting difference in gain confounds label
definition with composition, class mass and clustering.

**Measurement.** Conditioning gain is the relative improvement in held-out
perplexity from supplying the correct control token rather than a shuffled one,
with five independent shuffles per run. Shuffling rather than removing the
token is deliberate: it holds sequence length, token count and the model's
input distribution fixed, so the difference is attributable to the token's
*value* and not to its presence. We require a run to beat every shuffle and to
clear three standard deviations of the shuffle spread before its gain is
reported, and we report the same measurement twice -- once on 2,000 held-out
validation sequences and once on a cluster-disjoint test split -- so that a
figure quoted from one can be checked against the other. The test split's
cluster-disjointness is enforced at the clustering stage and verified in each
report.

**Setup.** ProGen2-base with LoRA, stage A, 3,000 steps on a single A10.
Seventeen runs: eight thresholds on the primary corpus (section 5.1), five on
a design-matched second corpus (5.2b), three on a second backbone (5.2c), and
four on an unselected third corpus (5.2d), with 30, 55 and 75 degrees C shared
between the first and third so they compare directly.

**Primary corpus.** 311,987 sequences, 113.8M residues, 47 distinct growth
temperature values, 1,552 both-class clusters at the 45 degrees C threshold,
279,980 training sequences. Growth temperatures from Gosha; sequences from
UniProt, species-selected and capped at 500 per species.

**Third corpus.** The same Swiss-Prot/Engqvist join with no per-species cap
and no balancing -- 204,039 sequences over 1,816 species and 64.9M residues,
the corpus as the databases have it. Screened for Cas9 homology (78 sequences
removed, the strictest at 96.2% identity to a held-out Cas9) and split
cluster-disjoint at 90/5/5: train 182,522 / val 10,176 / test 11,263. Its
Gate 2 runs on 5,000 validation sequences rather than 2,000, because the
subsample takes the first n and the first 2,000 of this split are 8.1%
thermophilic against its true 10.3%; test splits are scored in full on every
run in the paper.

**Second corpus.** UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot 2026_03 joined to Engqvist (BMC
Microbiology 18:177, 2018) instead of Gosha, then put through the same
species-balancing and 500-per-species cap: 35,361 sequences over 380 species
and 11.1M residues. Screened for Cas9 homology (11 sequences removed) and split
cluster-disjoint at 90/5/5, giving train 31,968 / val 1,687 / test 1,695 with
both classes present in both held-out splits. Both corpora are clustered at the
same identity and coverage thresholds, which is what makes the within-cluster
column comparable between them.

**Convergence.** `sweep_t45` reproduces the configured binning and reaches
+0.260% at 3,000 steps, against a 20,000-step baseline's +0.265%. Variant
comparisons are therefore comparisons of converged conditioning and not of how
far each run got in a fixed budget -- which would be a different and much less
interesting experiment.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Label definition is worth 3.9x in measured gain

Eight thresholds, one corpus, relabeling-only. Every run passed Gate 2 with z
between 6.7 and 20.8 against a required 3.0, so nothing in the measured column
is noise.

| edge (C) | corpus-wide | within-cluster | val gain | test gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | +0.095% | +0.066% | +0.037% | +0.033% |
| 35 | +0.198% | +0.114% | +0.104% | +0.087% |
| 45 | +0.154% | +0.172% | +0.260% | +0.251% |
| 50 | +0.190% | +0.168% | **+0.296%** | **+0.270%** |
| 55 | +0.257% | +0.176% | +0.255% | +0.238% |
| 65 | +0.184% | +0.140% | +0.282% | +0.251% |
| 70 | +0.161% | +0.106% | +0.160% | +0.152% |
| 75 | +0.118% | +0.081% | +0.121% | +0.119% |

The robust finding, and the one the rest of the paper rests on: measured gain
is a plateau from 45 to 65 degrees C (+0.255% to +0.296%) and falls away
sharply outside it, to +0.070% averaged over 30 and 35 degrees C. That is a
factor of 3.9 from label definition alone, on byte-identical sequences. Test
gains track validation within 0.03 pp at every threshold.

### 5.1b The envelope: a two-class threshold reaches a quarter of it

The sweeps above vary the threshold. This varies the *granularity*, up to the
limit, and gives the number a group with a corpus and no GPU budget can
compute: how much a growth-temperature label could be worth here at best.

| corpus | distinct OGTs | residues | 2-class | envelope | 2-class share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| primary (Gosha, capped + balanced) | 47 | 113.8M | +0.154% | **+0.661%** | 23.4% |
| external, design-matched | 63 | 11.1M | +0.274% | **+0.970%** | 28.3% |
| external, uncapped | 77 | 64.9M | +0.148% | **+0.488%** | 30.4% |

**The two-class threshold that every system in section 2 uses reaches 23% to
30% of what the corpus makes available to a label.** That holds across three
corpora, two growth-temperature databases, and a threefold range of absolute
envelopes. Miller-Madow corrections are 0.000, 0.005 and 0.000 percentage
points respectively.

Two cautions belong with this table, both in the text and not in a footnote.

**The envelope is a bound, not a target.** It bounds what a perfect unigram
predictor could take from the label; it says nothing about what a trained
model will. Our one granularity run reached +0.248% against a four-class bound
of +0.374%, which is 0.66x, and section 6.2 shows this measure cannot rank
granularities against each other at all. "Use more classes" does not follow
from this table and we do not claim it.

**The intermediate rows are not nested.** Equal-residue-mass edges for k and
k+1 classes are different partitions rather than refinements of one another,
so the series can dip -- it does at six and eleven classes on the uncapped
corpus. Monotonicity holds between a partition and its refinements, which is
what makes the native-resolution row an upper bound on all of them; it does
not hold along this particular sequence of binnings.

### 5.1c The statistic predicts it only when conditioned on family

| predictor | rho (val) | p | rho (test) | p | Pearson (val) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| corpus-wide | +0.381 | 0.180 | +0.359 | 0.190 | +0.615 |
| **within-cluster** | **+0.714** | **0.029** | **+0.743** | **0.021** | **+0.907** |
| `-abs(edge-55)`, no corpus | +0.843 | 0.007 | +0.849 | 0.006 | +0.906 |

p-values are exact one-sided permutation tests over all 40,320 relabelings.

Two things must be said plainly here, in this order.

**The corpus-wide form fails, and an earlier four-threshold subset of this same
experiment gave +0.80 for it.** That subset reproduces +0.800 exactly when the
other four runs are dropped, so this is not a computational correction; it is
what doubling n did to a coefficient that was never significant. The paper
should say so, in one sentence, because it is the cleanest available
demonstration of why the field's small-n conditioning claims need a denominator
in the first place -- including ours.

**Its largest error is diagnostic.** 35 degrees C ranks seventh of eight by
corpus-wide statistic and second by measured gain. It is also the most
class-balanced threshold in the corpus, H(bin) = 0.679 of a possible 0.693. The
residue histogram sees a large difference because the classes are even, but the
hot side at 35 degrees C is mostly 37 degrees C mesophiles, so the label is
semantically mush and the model cannot use it. Conditioning on family removes
most of this *on this corpus*: within-cluster reads +0.114% there, ranked
third.

The qualifier is not hedging. Section 5.2d shows the family-conditioned form
failing in exactly the same way on a corpus that was not species-balanced,
where both aggregations correlate +0.40 with H(bin) and measured gain
correlates **-0.80** with it. Per-species capping and species balancing break
up the pile-ups that make clusters internally balanced for empty reasons; a
corpus assembled without them does not get the rescue. What 5.1c establishes
is therefore narrower than it first appears: conditioning on family mitigates
the balance artifact on corpora built like ours, and the artifact is the
measure's dominant failure mode either way.

This is the within-cluster quantity's one real claim to credibility and it has
to be stated: it was not chosen after seeing these data. It is what
`conditioning_gate/ceiling_gain` has been configured on since 2026-10-26, and
the measure that computes it describes it as the token's value given the
family. The corpus-wide column was preferred for sweep comparisons only because
it carries no cluster-count caveat.

### 5.1d The baseline that reads nothing from the corpus beats both

`-abs(edge - 55)` scores above both aggregations here, and the paper concedes
it in the results rather than in the limitations.

The obvious defence -- that the baseline's constant was chosen knowing the
answer, that nothing computed from a new corpus tells you where to centre it,
and that section 5.3 shows the optimum is not always near 55 -- was tested
twice and does not survive. Section 5.2b's test was inconclusive because both
corpora's optima sat within 10 degrees C of 55. Section 5.2d's was
pre-registered on a corpus where the predictors disagree outright, and the
baseline won it: rank correlation **+1.000** against the corpus-wide
statistic's **+0.000**, with the pre-registered primary comparison negative at
6.6 and 7.1 sigma on the two splits.

So this is not an open problem that the paper flags and leaves. It is a
result:

> **No corpus statistic tested here ranks label definitions better than
> distance from the middle of the temperature range.** On the one corpus built
> to separate the two, the rule of thumb was perfect and the corpus-wide
> statistic was exactly uninformative.

What the statistic retains is weaker and should be stated with it: it tracks
the *magnitude* of achievable gain better than the baseline does on the
species-balanced corpora (Pearson +0.98 against +0.83, section 5.2b), and its
failure mode is predictable in advance from H(bin) rather than mysterious
(section 5.2d). A reader deciding whether to compute it deserves both halves.

### 5.2d The fixed-centre baseline wins: the pre-registered test, and its result

Section 5.2b's test of `-abs(edge - 55)` failed on its design: both corpora's
measured optima landed within 10 degrees C of the baseline's constant, so it
was never exposed. This is the replacement, on the uncapped corpus of section
5.3, whose bound peaks at 35 degrees C. Four runs at 30, 35, 45 and 60 degrees
C. The design, the primary outcome, the secondary analysis and two void
conditions were written into this section and committed **before the runs were
launched**; what follows reports them in the order they were fixed. Full
analysis in `results/ceiling_sweep_20261030/uncapped_measured.md`.

The corpus was screened for Cas9 homology before it became training data: 78
of 204,039 sequences removed at >30% identity over >=50% coverage to a held-out
Cas9, the strictest at 96.2%. (The design-matched corpus lost 11 of 35,361;
this one is six times the size and unselected, so it contains real Cas9
orthologs, and training on it unscreened would have left the stage B leakage
gate passing while measuring memorisation.) It was re-clustered and split
cluster-disjoint at 90/5/5 -- train 182,522 / val 10,176 / test 11,263, both
classes in both held-out splits -- and the bound sweep re-run on the screened
corpus.

**The predictors disagree here, and this was verified before launch rather
than asserted.** Over these four thresholds Spearman between the corpus-wide
bound and the baseline is **0.0**, and between the within-cluster bound and
the baseline **+0.2**. Over section 5.2b's edges both were +0.9, which is why
that test could not separate them.

| 30 | 68.8% | 0.6205 | +0.113% | +0.100% | +0.035% | +0.038% | 0.31x | **failed (size)** |
| 35 | 49.4% | 0.6931 | **+0.148%** | **+0.119%** | +0.065% | +0.061% | 0.44x | **failed (size)** |

**Primary outcome: negative on both splits.** The pre-registered statistic is

baseline predict opposite orderings.

|---|---|---|---|---|

**Neither void condition is met, and the letter of them is what counts.** The
four gains span 0.113 pp against a largest two-sigma of 0.025 pp, so condition
(i) fails. Every run cleared the significance criterion at z between 10.9 and
40.7, so condition (ii) fails. `unc_t30` and `unc_t35` did fail Gate 2, but on
the **size** criterion, which condition (ii) does not name -- it names
significance, because a gain indistinguishable from zero cannot rank anything,
and these are distinguishable from zero at ten to forty sigma. Reading "failed
Gate 2" as voiding the test would be a reinterpretation made after seeing an
unfavourable result, and it is not available.

#### Why: the balance artifact survives family conditioning

| | Spearman with H(bin) |
|---|---|
| corpus-wide bound | +0.400 |
| within-cluster bound | +0.400 |
| measured gain (val) | **-0.800** |
| measured gain (test) | **-0.600** |

Pearson between H(bin) and measured validation gain is **-0.926**. Mutual
information is maximised at an even marginal, so both bounds rise with
balance; on this corpus measured gain falls with it. The two are
anti-correlated through class balance, which is sufficient to produce a rank
correlation of zero.

The cause is the 37 degrees C pile-up. At 30 and 35 degrees C the corpus is
68.8% and 49.4% thermophilic, but the hot class is overwhelmingly Swiss-Prot's
clinical and model organisms annotated at exactly 37. The label is nearly
perfectly balanced and semantically empty: it separates "37 degrees C" from
"not 37 degrees C". A residue histogram sees a large composition difference
between two arbitrary halves of a corpus; the model finds 0.31x and 0.44x of
it usable, the two worst ratios in this work and the reason both runs failed
Gate 2 on size. At 45 and 60 degrees C the hot class is 9.2% and 6.4% and
genuinely thermophilic: the bound is *lower*, because the marginal is
lopsided, and measured gain is higher.

**This narrows section 5.1c.** Conditioning on family removes most of the
balance artifact on the two species-balanced corpora, where per-species
capping breaks up exactly this kind of pile-up. It does not remove it here,
because when half a corpus is 37 degrees C organisms a great many clusters are
internally balanced at a 35 degrees C threshold for the same empty reason. The
family-conditioned rescue is a property of corpus design, not of the
aggregation.

### 5.3 Sampling design is half the measurement

The uncapped, unbalanced external corpus peaks at 35 degrees C instead, and
agreement with stage A drops to Spearman +0.582. The cause is in the class
masses: between 35 and 40 degrees C the hot class loses four fifths of its
residues, 49.4% to 9.5%, because Swiss-Prot is dominated by clinical and model
organisms annotated at 37 degrees C and holds almost nothing between 37 and 45.
The 35 degrees C peak is that cliff.

So the bound's peak location is a joint property of the corpus and the
species-selection design, and a ceiling quoted without the design is not
reproducible. We prefer to own this as a result rather than have it found: it
is the main thing a practitioner needs to know before computing the measure on
their own data.

### 5.4 What the 45 degrees C convention actually costs: much less than the statistic predicts

The corpus statistic puts the convention well below the optimum in all three
corpora -- 1.67x, 1.48x and 1.26x. Measured, it is not:

| | predicted by statistic | measured |
|---|---|---|
| optimum | 55 C | 50 C |
| gain at the optimum | +0.257% | +0.296% |
| gain at 45 C | +0.154% | +0.260% |
| shortfall of the convention | 1.67x | **1.14x** |

And 1.14x is roughly 2 sigma on the shuffle spread, with 65 degrees C (+0.282%)
within noise of both. So the convention sits at the low edge of the measured
plateau and retuning inside the plateau buys little.

This is a correction the paper has to make in its own voice, because the
earlier ceiling-based version of it was the most quotable thing here. What
replaces it is narrower and still useful:

- **Getting the threshold badly wrong is expensive** -- 3.9x between a cold
  threshold and the plateau. That is the claim with margin to spare.
- **Getting it slightly wrong is cheap.** 45 degrees C is nearly optimal on
  this corpus, and anyone who has used the convention has lost about 14%.
- **The statistic was wrong about the size of the available improvement**, in
  the direction that would have motivated unnecessary work. A measure that
  overstates its own payoff has to report that.

### 5.5 The bound is not only class balance

Mutual information is largest when the marginal is even, so the obvious
objection is that a threshold sweep finds the median growth temperature by an
expensive route. The sweep reports $H(b)$ and $I/H$ so this is answerable from
its output.

| corpus | Spearman(H, ceiling) | most balanced edge | peak | balance explains it? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| stage A | +0.545 | 35 C | 55 C | no |
| external, natural | +0.664 | 35 C | 35 C | **yes** |
| external, design-matched | +0.627 | 40 C | 60 C | no |

Balance correlates with the bound everywhere and determines the peak only in
the corpus with the 37 degrees C cliff. In both designed corpora the peak sits
15 to 20 degrees C above the most balanced threshold, so something other than
the marginal carries it: at hot thresholds the two classes' residue
compositions differ more, which is what the measure is for.

$I/H$ rises monotonically toward hot thresholds in all three corpora, peaking
at 70, 75 and 65 degrees C. That is consistent cross-corpus structure and it is
not the quantity that predicted measured gain, so it stays an observation.

## 6. Where the measure fails

This section is not a limitations paragraph bolted on at the end. For a
measurement paper it is a contribution, and it should be a numbered section
with its own results.

### 6.1 It ranks, loosely; it does not estimate

Corpus-wide, `gain / statistic` spans 0.39x to 1.69x, a 4.3x spread. A variant
can land at 40% of the figure or at 170% of it. Exceeding it is permitted --
the quantity is unigram and the model has context -- but a predictor undershot
by 2.6x in one variant and overshot by 1.7x in another estimates nothing.

Within-cluster is tighter. The six thresholds at and above 45 degrees C span
1.45x to 2.01x, a factor of 1.4, with the two cold thresholds below 1x. That
looks close to a fixed multiple of about 1.6 over the plateau, and it would be a
calibrated predictor if it held. It must be reported as an observation and not
a result, for a reason worth stating in the paper: the eight thresholds were
split into "plateau" and "cold" after the table was in hand, and a ratio that
tight across six post-hoc-selected points is exactly the shape a small sample
produces by chance. It was a hypothesis for the external corpora to test, and
they did not support it: the design-matched corpus gives 0.88x to 1.84x and the
uncapped one 0.31x to 1.29x, the latter spanning a factor of 4.2 across four
thresholds. The within-cluster form does not estimate either.

### 6.2 It does not rank across granularities, and the obvious fix is worse

`sweep_c4` has the highest bound of the five by a wide margin, +0.374%, 1.5x
the 55 degrees C threshold's, and a *lower* measured gain than both t45 and
t55. Four classes raise the bound without raising what the model extracts, so a
single number does not order label definitions that differ in kind.

The natural repair is to normalize by label entropy, since refinement raises
both $I$ and $H$. It makes things worse. Spearman against measured gain goes
from +0.600 raw to -0.300 normalized across all five runs, and from +0.800 to
-0.200 on the four thresholds. We report this as a dead end rather than omit
it. Whether the granularity axis needs its own bound, or whether the gap is a
capacity limit on a single control token rather than a corpus property, is open.

### 6.3 There is no peak to locate, only a plateau

At n=4 this section would have said the peak is interior but unresolved. At
n=8 the shape is clearer and different: 45, 50, 55 and 65 degrees C lie within
0.041 pp of each other, which is about 2 sigma end to end, so there is no
identifiable optimum. "Measured gain plateaus between 45 and 65 degrees C" is
the strongest true statement, and the corpus limits resolution further -- 47
distinct growth-temperature values across 311,987 rows with an 8 degrees C gap
between 37 and 45.

This has a consequence worth drawing out rather than burying, because it
weakens the practical value of any predictor: if the useful region is a wide
plateau, then the precision a corpus statistic could add over a rule of thumb
is small, and the thing worth predicting is which thresholds fall *off* the
plateau. Both forms of the statistic get the cold end right.

### 6.4 Scope

Prokaryotes only; Engqvist's 7,682 eukaryote rows are excluded, as are the
299,890 Swiss-Prot entries whose organism is absent from it. Two backbones,
both ProGen2 decoders with the same tokenizer and conditioning scheme, so
section 5.2c is a capacity comparison and not an architecture-independence
result. Measured gain is validated on three corpora: two assembled by the same
species-balancing and per-species capping design (5.2b), and the uncapped
corpus of 5.3 and 5.2d, which is the one that breaks the statistic. Two of the
three therefore share a sampling design, and the conclusions that depend on
design -- the family-conditioned rescue in 5.1c above all -- rest on those two.
The two growth-temperature databases both
scrape overlapping culture-collection catalogues (DSMZ, ATCC, JCM), so their
agreement is evidence the bound is not an artifact of one *corpus* and not
evidence that it survives an independent *measurement* of growth temperature.
The design-matched external corpus is a tenth of stage A's residue mass and
hotter at matched thresholds (41.3% thermophilic at 55 degrees C against stage
A's 28.0%), so its larger measured gains are not evidence that a smaller corpus
conditions better. Its within-cluster figures at 75 and 80 degrees C rest on 72
and 59 clusters; the within-cluster column is used for the 30-65 degrees C
comparisons in 5.2b, where cluster counts are 125 to 156, and the corpus-wide
column elsewhere. On the uncapped corpus the within-cluster figures at 45 and
60 degrees C rest on 302 and 186 clusters, the thinnest used for any claim
here, and its held-out hot classes at those thresholds are roughly 1,000 and
700 sequences.

## 7. A worked negative case: Cas9

The practical use of a corpus-only measure is a go/no-go decision taken before
the budget is spent. This section is one, and the decision was no.

We assembled a Cas9 corpus for exactly the conditioning experiment described
above: 1,490 candidates from six Cas9-specific Pfam families and a
protein-name query, 1,444 carrying both the RuvC and HNH domains at i-Evalue
<= 1e-3, 1,019 joined to a growth temperature, 208 clusters at 45% identity and
80% coverage. By the standards of a protein family it is a reasonable corpus.
By the standards of this experiment it is unusable, and three cheap checks say
so independently:

| gate | outcome |
|---|---|
| Gate 1, sequences per class | **fail** -- 23 thermophilic against a floor of 200 |
| Gate 2, measurable conditioning | **not evaluable** -- the test split holds zero thermophilic sequences, so shuffling the token across it is a no-op by construction |
| Leakage | **fail** -- 222 pairs over 21 held-out sequences above 45% identity and 80% coverage, despite cluster-disjoint splitting |
| Corpus-wide bound | +0.008%, against the primary corpus's +0.154% |

Three observations, and the second and third are the ones worth the page.

The bound is 19x smaller than the primary corpus's, and the gates fail
independently of it. Agreement among three cheap checks is what makes the
decision easy: no single measurement has to be trusted on its own, and the
whole assessment cost a CPU afternoon against the roughly $3 and 2.6 hours a
single GPU run costs -- or, had it been missed, against a stage B fine-tuning
result that could not have been interpreted.

**Gate 2 was not evaluable, which is a different outcome from failing.** With
no thermophilic sequences in the test split, shuffling the growth-temperature
token across it changes nothing and the measured gain is approximately zero
whatever the model learned. A pipeline that reported that zero as a result
would have produced a confident negative finding with no content. We now assert
in the test suite that every split of every trainable corpus carries both
classes, with this corpus named in the failure message.

**The within-cluster figure for this corpus is +0.119%, and it is not usable.**
Taken at face value it looks within reach of the primary corpus's +0.172%, and
a reader who wanted to run the experiment could quote it. It rests on three
clusters and 57,385 residues. Mutual information estimated from small counts is
upward-biased, three clusters is not a sample, and the honest statement is that
this corpus cannot measure its own ceiling. We recorded the number and refused
to configure it. A paper whose subject is measurement should say where its own
measurement is untrustworthy, and this is the clearest such place.

One general warning from the leakage failure: MMseqs2's greedy set-cover
clustering guarantees dissimilar cluster *representatives*, not dissimilar
*members* across clusters. Cluster-disjoint splitting is necessary and is not
sufficient, and 222 leaking pairs over 21 held-out sequences is how emphatically
it can fail on a single tight family.

## 8. Discussion

**Label definition is an untuned free parameter and it is cheap to tune.** That
is the finding we would most like a reader to act on, and it does not depend on
the corpus statistic working -- which, on the evidence below, it largely does
not. Across seventeen runs, the choice of threshold moves measured
conditioning gain by a factor of 3.9 on the primary corpus, 3.8 on the
design-matched one and 4.2 on the uncapped one, with the cold end of the range
losing most of the available signal in all three. Every system in section 2
fixes this parameter by convention.

Four recommendations, in descending order of how well this paper supports them.

**Do not define the label at a cold threshold.** This is the robust result. On
all three corpora and both backbones, thresholds at or below 35 degrees C give
a small fraction of the gain available above 40. The trap is specific and
worth naming: if a corpus is dominated by organisms annotated at 37 degrees C,
as any unselected reviewed-protein corpus is, a threshold just below 37 splits
it almost perfectly evenly and produces a label whose hot class is mostly
mesophiles. It will look like the best-balanced choice and, on our uncapped
corpus, it returned a third of the gain of a 60 degrees C threshold.

**The conventional 45 degrees C threshold is nearly optimal, so do not retune
it expecting much.** Our measured optimum is 50 degrees C at 1.14x over 45,
roughly 2 sigma, and 65 degrees C is within noise of both: the measured
structure is a plateau from 45 to 65, not a peak. This corrects an expectation
our own corpus statistic created -- it predicted a 1.67x gap between 45 and 55
where the measured gap is 1.02x in the other direction -- and it is a case where
the measure overstated what retuning was worth.

**Report a likelihood-level conditioning gain, not only a downstream delta.**
This is the recommendation we would most like adopted, and PRIME's ablation is
why. Removing its temperature objective costs 0.106 Spearman on
thermostability assays and nothing on activity assays -- a careful experiment
whose result no other group can use, because the units are downstream,
unbounded above and specific to an assay set. One evaluation pass with the
label shuffled gives a number in the same units as the corpus statistic, costs
a fraction of a training run, and is comparable across papers. Shuffling
rather than removing matters: it holds sequence length and input distribution
fixed, so the difference is attributable to the label's value rather than its
presence.

**Report the envelope and the label entropy beside it.** A gain of X% means
nothing alone; X% against what the corpus allows is comparable, and the
envelope (section 5.1b) is computable from residue counts without a GPU. Our
own results are the reason to report H(bin) with it: the bound inflates
wherever the label splits the corpus evenly, so a gain quoted as a fraction of
it is flattered on balanced thresholds and penalised on lopsided ones. The
entropy costs nothing and makes the inflation visible. It is the diagnostic we
would want from someone else's paper and the one we would have wanted from our
own, earlier.

**Hold the species-selection design fixed and report it.** The location of the
optimum is a joint property of the corpus and the sampling design (section
5.3), and a bound quoted without the design is not reproducible. This is the
easiest thing in this paper to get wrong, and we got it wrong once: our own
uncapped corpus appeared to peak at 35 degrees C, which turned out to be
Swiss-Prot's 37 degrees C pile-up rather than anything about thermophilic
sequence.

**Do not use this statistic to choose the threshold.** We set out to supply a
denominator and we would have preferred to recommend one. On the corpus chosen
in advance to give it its best chance, against a baseline that reads nothing
from any corpus, it ranked four label definitions no better than chance
(+0.000) while the baseline ranked them perfectly (+1.000), with the
pre-registered comparison negative at more than six sigma. A reader who wants
a threshold is better served by "well above the mode of your temperature
distribution" than by computing our measure. Where it does earn its cost is as
a *scale*: on the species-balanced corpora it tracks the magnitude of
achievable gain well (Pearson +0.98 against the baseline's +0.83), and that is
the use the field actually lacks -- comparing a reported gain against what its
corpus allowed, rather than choosing a label.

**The strongest follow-up needs no new training.** Compute the bound for the
published setups' own corpora. The Engqvist data behind PRIME's ~96M pairs is
public, as is the Swiss-Prot side of it. "Their reported gain is X% of what
their corpus allows" would make this measure useful to everyone already working
in this area rather than only to us, and it is a CPU job. Beyond that: a third
corpus with a growth-temperature measurement genuinely independent of the
culture-collection catalogues that Gosha and Engqvist both scrape, and a second
architecture family to turn section 5.2c from a capacity comparison into an

**Figure 1** (`figure1_bound_vs_measured`) -- two panels, same y axis:

*Caption.* Measured conditioning gain against the corpus-only bound, under two
aggregations of the same statistic, on three corpora. Conditioning on sequence
family (right) rescues the statistic on the two species-balanced corpora --
Spearman +0.714 and +1.000, against +0.381 and +0.900 corpus-wide -- and does
not rescue it on the unselected corpus, where it reaches +0.200 against
+0.000. On that corpus the bound and the measurement are anti-correlated

**Figure 2** (`figure2_bound_vs_threshold`) -- the bound against threshold for

The second row is the point of the figure: the uncapped corpus's peak sits

*Caption.* The corpus-only bound against binning threshold, for the primary
corpus and two forms of an independently assembled second corpus. Under matched
species selection the two designed corpora agree in shape and peak within one
sweep step (Spearman +0.809). The uncapped corpus peaks at 35 degrees C
instead, which the lower panel explains: between 35 and 40 degrees C its hot
class loses four fifths of its mass to Swiss-Prot's 37 degrees C pile-up. The
peak's location is a joint property of the corpus and the sampling design.

**Figure 3** (`figure3_measured_vs_threshold`) -- measured gain against
threshold for all four trained configurations, with the primary corpus's

writing the caption that three of the four were run at a subset of the
thresholds, so their line segments join measured points across gaps and
interpolate nothing.

*Caption.* Measured conditioning gain against binning threshold, on three

## Standing cautions for the writing

- The bound is a **lower** bound. No saturation claims, anywhere, in any
  phrasing.
- Structure prediction is a validity filter. It is not a thermostability assay
  and no figure title may imply otherwise.
- Growth temperature and sampling temperature stay separate in every name,
  caption and sentence.
- The auxiliary OGT head is PRIME's.
- `sweep_t45` was launched with `--ceiling-gain 0.00172`, the within-cluster
  figure, so its own Gate 2 report prints 1.51x where the corpus-wide column
  gives 1.69x. The flag sets only that printed denominator; it does not touch
  training or the measured gain. Every number in section 5.1 uses the
  corpus-wide column for all five runs. If the per-run reports go into an
  appendix, this needs a footnote there.
